In Puyo Puyo Tetris 2, you rotate and move falling Puyos. Connecting at least four of one color makes the connected group disappear. Watch the matching color on the left board and the space it leaves behind.

Choosing where to place the next piece begins with recognizing which Puyos belong together. If their colors are difficult to distinguish, that first decision becomes harder. The rule stays the same, but the clues used to read it can look different to different players.

We will change the colors in the same scene to see what disappears and what remains. First we will strengthen the clues that distinguish types, apply that idea to Mario’s items and paths, and then turn a Unity scene grayscale to examine the order of attention.

For a clear demonstration, we made two types of pieces with the same shape. Red pieces belong with red pieces, and green pieces with green pieces. Distinct colors let us quickly group matching types even when they are separated.

Now keep their positions and shapes unchanged and reduce only the difference between the two colors. It becomes harder to tell which pieces match. The pieces have not disappeared; the clue identifying their type has weakened.

This can be easy to miss when inspecting two isolated pieces. During play, many pieces stack together and background artwork remains visible. When a theme changes the colors of the pieces and their surroundings, check whether the same types remain recognizable in that combination.

Color-vision simulation helps identify these problems in advance. It previews particular color-vision conditions so we can check whether different pieces remain distinguishable from one another and from their background.

Photoshop’s color proofing includes Protanopia-type and Deuteranopia-type options. They preview different conditions. Instead of stopping after one view looks acceptable, check whether matching pieces and their boundaries remain recognizable in each.

Keep this separate from a grayscale check. Grayscale removes hue so we can examine light and dark relationships. It is not an exact reproduction of a particular color-vision condition. Color-vision simulation focuses on color distinctions; grayscale focuses on brightness relationships.

Puyo Puyo Tetris 2 also added color-adjustment options, allowing players to choose among several filters. This combines checks during development with options for the player. How do we repair a distinction that still does not read clearly?

Return to our two pieces. Even with a small color difference, a light piece and a dark piece can remain distinguishable. This difference in lightness is a lightness contrast. Keep shape and position fixed and increase only that difference.

The two pieces are easier to distinguish now. But place a dark background behind the darker piece: although the pieces differ, its edge can blend into its surroundings. Check both the distinction between the two types and each piece’s separation from its background.

Making the background lighter or adding a light outline restores that boundary. Rather than making every piece brighter, compare the two areas that actually touch.

Now add clues that survive independently of color. Give one type a circle, another a triangle, and use stripes where helpful. Repeat the same marking for the same type. Three circles plus another circle make a group of four—a relationship the player can follow even when color is difficult to read.

Restore the distinct colors without removing these markings. Color helps players find matching types quickly, while shape and pattern confirm that judgment. The same information now has more than one route to the player.

The principle also applies outside puzzle games. Look at the flowers and question-mark blocks in Super Mario Bros. Wonder. They are not merely two colored spots: flower-petal outlines, a square block and a question-mark symbol provide different clues.

Mario moves beneath the block and jumps. Hitting it releases an item, which Mario then collects. Recognizing the object connects to a concrete action. Players likewise learn a flower’s role through the response when they approach it.

A distinctive color helps draw attention from a distance. Up close, outlines and symbols confirm the object, and the result of interacting with it teaches its meaning. When color, shape and action all point in the same direction, unfamiliar scenes become easier to read.

Once an object is recognizable, consider what the player should notice first. In our Unity scene, a path and a treasure chest lie in front of the character, with trees behind them. Keep the camera and every object fixed and remove only the color.

In color, the green trees, brown path and chest had distinct hues. In grayscale those hue labels disappear, leaving light and dark masses. If the path and nearby ground have similar brightness, their boundary weakens. If the distant trees are brighter, they can draw attention backward.

First, reduce the range of light and dark in the background trees. Next, separate the path’s edge and the chest’s outline from their surroundings. We have not removed objects or moved the camera, yet the route and goal in front of the character are easier to see.

Now restore the color. Hue differences reinforce a path already readable through brightness. The chest becomes easier to locate through its distinct color, while the background remains secondary. The aim is not to remove color, but to give it a clearer foundation for communicating information.

Ask the same question in Mario’s scene: should the eye go first to the ground beneath the character, the next obstacle, or the distant scenery? Arranging emphasis around the decisions needed for play is visual hierarchy.

Color-vision simulation checks whether matching types remain distinguishable. Grayscale checks brightness, boundaries and the order of attention. Neither replaces the other: both ask whether the same game information remains readable under different viewing conditions.

Flipping artwork can reveal awkward details hidden by familiarity. Temporarily removing color is another way to see a scene afresh. Finally, check whether players with different color vision can actually locate the same objects and act on them.

Back at the opening puzzle, color is a powerful way to group matching types quickly. Supporting it with lightness, shape and pattern keeps the information available to people who perceive color differently. Can players find the object, separate it from the background and choose their next action? Those are the three questions to ask when refining a game’s colors.
