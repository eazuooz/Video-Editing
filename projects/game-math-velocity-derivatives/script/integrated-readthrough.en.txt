In Super Mario Odyssey, Mario crosses a room, follows the wall, and returns toward its middle. Can the starting and finishing positions alone tell us how far he traveled or how his motion changed? We will record position and time with consistent units, move from an interval average to the rate of change at one instant, then combine path slope and travel speed to calculate the rate of gaining height. First, let us decide what to record from the motion we just saw.

The first question is where the object was at each time. That is a question of kinematics. Dynamics asks why it moved and which forces caused the motion. Here we represent the object as a particle with a position and temporarily set aside heat, electricity, fluids, and its own orientation or rotation. Our goal is to connect the meaning of differentiation to the simple calculation rules needed for motion.

A simplified model does not make the omitted ideas useless. Energy conservation can simplify motion problems and reveal a simulation that gains speed for no physical reason. Newtonian formulations emphasize forces, while Lagrangian formulations use energy-based quantities. We begin with the relation between position and time. The choice of formulation does not establish a universal rule that one handles friction better in every problem.

We use a mathematical model in which time and position vary continuously. A computer evaluates it at discrete frames, but the equation describing the motion can still be continuous. We do not need a conclusion about the fundamental structure of spacetime. We assume a shared time coordinate, Euclidean space, and specified initial conditions and motion rules. Errors caused by finite time steps or numerical precision belong to the implementation and must be distinguished from the model.

A particle in this model has mass but no size or orientation. That does not mean a character literally becomes a point; it means we track the position needed for the question being asked. We can understand motion along one line and then extend the calculation to horizontal, vertical, and depth components. Rotation and collision shape require additional information when those properties become relevant.

Before recording a position, we need consistent units. Mechanics uses length, time, and mass as basic quantities; we will generally use meters, seconds, and kilograms. Mass measures inertia, the resistance to changing motion. Weight is a gravitational force and changes with the gravitational field. Mass is measured in kilograms and weight in newtons. Even in a game with constant gravity, substituting one for the other breaks force calculations.

Combining the basic quantities gives the units of other quantities. Area is length times length, measured in square meters, and volume uses three length factors. Velocity divides displacement by time, giving meters per second. Acceleration divides a change in velocity by time once more, giving meters per second squared. The symbols L, T, and M describe these dimensions independently of the particular measurement units.

To find how often an object passes the same point, count repetitions over a time interval. Dividing by elapsed time gives frequency. Hertz means cycles per second, and a kilohertz is a thousand hertz. The prefix kilo takes a lowercase k. Force combines mass and acceleration, work combines force and displacement along it, and power divides work by time. Following the units shows why multiplying by another length cannot still give energy.

Tracking units is a way to check whether a formula really returns a change in position. Multiply velocity by elapsed time and the time unit cancels, leaving a length. Convert a frame duration in milliseconds to seconds first. Otherwise the same car jumps far too far in one frame. Comparing the intended quantity with the units left by the formula is dimensional analysis.

Measure the same stretch of track first in metres and then in feet. The spacing of the marks changes while the two ends of the track stay where they are. Multiply by a ratio of equivalent lengths to preserve the physical distance. Put the unit to remove in the denominator so metres cancel and feet remain.

Speed contains a length and a time, so both scales have to agree. Follow the same car in kilometres per hour, then read its speed in feet per second. On the length side convert kilometres to metres and metres to feet. On the time side use seconds per hour to cancel hours. The car keeps moving continuously while feet remain in the numerator and seconds in the denominator.

With units settled, return to motion. A hare runs quickly but sometimes moves backward or rests, while a tortoise advances slowly and steadily. A position-versus-time graph separates being fast at a particular moment from arriving first. Time is horizontal, and signed position is vertical. A high point means the runner is farther along; a steep segment means position is changing rapidly.

We want to know how quickly and in which direction position changed on average over an interval. Join the start and end positions with an arrow. Subtracting start from end gives this displacement. Delta means a change. Delta x is the displacement between the points and delta t is the time between them. Displacement divided by elapsed time is average velocity. Move the endpoint farther in the same time and the average velocity in that direction grows.

This ratio is the slope of the straight line joining the two points on the graph. It divides the vertical change by the horizontal interval. Even when the hare slows down, the line between the endpoints gives one average velocity for the whole interval. That number does not describe every event in between, so its start and end times must always be specified.

Now follow someone who walks out and returns to the start. Start and finish coincide, so displacement and average velocity are zero. Yet the path that was walked is still visible. Total path length divided by elapsed time gives average speed. The tortoise and hare can have the same average velocity between common endpoints and times while moving quite differently in between.

Watch the speedometer as the car pulls away. Its position advances along the road while the gauge shows how fast it is moving at that instant. An average over the road just covered does not tell us how speed changed along the way. How could we find the current speed on a graph? At a single instant both changes would be zero. Take a short interval starting at that instant and shrink its width instead. A straight line has the same slope everywhere. On a curve, shorter intervals reveal the local motion more clearly.

Let's model the slowing hare with a position curve. The horizontal axis is time in minutes and the vertical axis is position in furlongs. Place the yellow point at the instant whose speed we want. The model is a quadratic whose slope decreases with time. Joining the point to a slightly later one still gives the average velocity over an interval.

Watch how a line through two points can tell us about velocity at one instant. Keep the first point fixed and pull the second towards it along the curve. As the horizontal time interval shrinks, so does the vertical difference. Their ratio, the slope of the line, approaches a definite value. The value that this slope approaches is the one we want.

We have not actually divided by a zero time interval. We examined the value approached as a nonzero interval tends to zero. That approached value is the limit. The secant joining two points tends toward the tangent at one point, and the tangent slope is instantaneous velocity. On a computer, making the interval excessively small can amplify floating-point error, but the mathematical definition uses the limit.

This definition is not limited to position. A derivative describes how rapidly an output changes when its input changes. Differentiating position with respect to time gives a velocity function. The result is not necessarily a single number: first we obtain a new function that returns velocity for a supplied time, then substitute a time such as 2.5 minutes to obtain a particular value. Distinguish the rate-of-change function from its value at one instant.

The same position can be crossed in different directions. Follow an object that slows down. At the top of the position graph the tangent is horizontal and velocity is zero. After the peak it moves left even while still to the right of the starting point. Its velocity graph drops below zero. That is why total distance travelled and displacement from the start differ.

The same tool measures changes other than motion. The derivative of accumulated rain-gauge height describes how quickly rain is accumulating now. Tank pressure can also be recorded over time. If temperature and volume are fixed and the ideal-gas approximation applies, pressure is proportional to the amount of gas, so its rate of change indicates the rate of inflow or outflow. That interpretation depends on those conditions.

The input need not be time. If terrain height is a function of horizontal position, its derivative gives the local slope: positive uphill, negative downhill, and zero on level ground. Now consider the proportion of climbers who reach a specified height. That proportion decreases with height, so its derivative is negative. A large negative magnitude indicates many climbers dropping out near that height; a value near zero indicates few.

If fewer climbers drop out after a rest ledge, the negative derivative moves closer to zero; its numerical value increases rather than decreases. A change from minus ten to minus two is an increase. Actual counts form steps, so this interpretation uses a smooth approximation. A hypothetical satisfaction-versus-income curve works similarly: its derivative measures the effect of one additional unit of income, an example of marginal utility. The illustrative curve is not survey evidence.

These examples share one definition. Call the current time t and a nearby time t plus h, so h is the time interval. Divide the change in position by h to get the average velocity over that short interval. If this ratio approaches a single value as h approaches zero, that limit defines instantaneous velocity at t. We do not substitute zero into the denominator. We take the limit of ratios formed with nonzero intervals.

Apply that definition to our position function, minus t squared plus six t minus one. To evaluate the position at t plus h, replace every t in the function with t plus h. Then subtract the entire original position at t. The minus sign before the second bracket applies to every term inside it. Keep h nonzero while simplifying.

Expanding the square cancels the t-squared terms, the six-t terms, and the constants. The numerator becomes minus two t h minus h squared plus six h. Every term contains h, so for nonzero h we can cancel it against the denominator. What remains is minus two t minus h plus six. Taking h toward zero now gives minus two t plus six: the velocity function for any time in this model.

Move a single time marker to check the velocity function against the curve. As the marker advances, the position tangent flattens and the point on the velocity graph moves towards zero. Beyond the peak the tangent tilts down and velocity turns negative. At our original instant it agrees with the slope found by shrinking the interval.

A position graph now tells us about velocity. A horizontal section means rest, an increasing section means positive velocity, and a decreasing section means negative velocity. A straight segment has constant slope and constant velocity; a curved segment has changing velocity. A sharp corner is different: if left and right slopes disagree, the derivative does not exist there. An idealized instantaneous change of direction in a game must handle that discontinuity separately.

There is another important loss of information. Moving a position graph vertically does not change its slope. Two objects that start at positions zero and ten but move identically have the same velocity function. Differentiation retains the rate of change but removes a constant position offset. That is why recovering position from velocity later requires a starting position.

You will see several notations for the same operation. d x over d t means differentiating position x with respect to time t. The operator d over d t placed before a function tells us to differentiate that function. A prime also denotes a derivative, while a dot above x often means a time derivative in mechanics. For a general function of x, we write d y over d x. The variable below tells us which input is changing.

We do not need to repeat a limit calculation for every function. Standard differentiation rules let us break complicated expressions into simpler parts. Start with a constant function. If its value remains k as time passes, its graph is horizontal and its derivative is zero. Whether k is three or one hundred makes no difference. The question is whether the value changes with the input.

In Tears of the Kingdom, Link stands beside the raft’s mast while the riverbank passes by. He is nearly still relative to the raft but moving relative to the shore. The reference used to record position matters. The derivative of a sum equals the sum of the derivatives. Imagine a person walking forward inside a train traveling along a straight line. Relative to the ground, the person’s position is the train’s position plus their position inside the train. Their ground velocity is therefore the sum of the two velocities: ten meters per second for the train plus two inside it gives twelve. Here we use aligned, nonrotating axes along a straight track.

If a function is multiplied by a constant, its derivative is multiplied by the same constant. Converting a position from furlongs to meters multiplies it by two hundred one point one six eight. The derived velocity gets the same conversion factor. Preserving sums and constant multiples is called linearity. It lets us differentiate a polynomial one term at a time and then add the results.

For t to the power n, multiply by n and lower the exponent by one. The derivative of t cubed is three t squared, and that of t squared is two t. For a quartic polynomial, apply the rule to each power and remove the constant term. The rule also works for negative integer powers on their domains, but a denominator involving t excludes zero where the original function is undefined.

Apply those rules in order. Three t to the fifth minus four t becomes fifteen t to the fourth minus four. For t to the hundredth divided by one hundred, the factor one hundred from differentiating cancels the denominator; the added square root of pi is constant and disappears. For reciprocals, rewrite the powers as negative exponents. The derivative of t to minus one is minus one over t squared, while four t to minus three becomes minus twelve over t to the fourth.

Games also use sine and cosine for rotations and oscillations. Maclaurin series connect these functions to polynomials near zero. Sine begins with x, minus x cubed over three factorial, plus x to the fifth over five factorial, and so on. A factorial multiplies the positive integers up to its argument: three factorial is six and five factorial is one hundred twenty. The angle x must be in radians.

Overlay the series approximation on the sine curve to see how closely they agree. Near zero the first few terms leave only a small gap. With those terms fixed, moving farther from zero can widen the gap. At the same input, watch what adding terms does to the gap. Over this range the next term bounds the error. This connects trigonometric functions to polynomials. Such a series does not represent every function, and actual libraries may evaluate sine differently.

Differentiate the sine series term by term. x becomes one. Minus x cubed over three factorial becomes minus x squared over two factorial. The fifth-power term brings down a five that cancels the five in five factorial, leaving four factorial. The resulting series is the cosine series. Differentiating cosine gives minus sine. With radians, sine’s slope is cosine and cosine’s slope is minus sine.

The exponential function has a related property. The series for e to the x begins with one, x, x squared over two factorial, and so on. Differentiating removes the first constant, but shifts every remaining term forward, recreating the original series. The derivative of e to the x is therefore itself. This is why the function appears in continuous growth models whose rate is proportional to the current amount.

For a quantity P growing continuously at a constant proportional rate r, its value is the initial amount times e to the r t. Here r is the growth rate per unit time. This is a mathematical continuous-compounding model, not a statement about a particular financial product. Nor is e to the x the only function equal to its derivative: any constant multiple also has that property. An initial value selects one specific solution.

So far the function’s input has been x itself. A vehicle traveling over a hill has a nested dependence: time changes its position along the track, and that position determines its height. Let s be a path coordinate measured along the track, positive in a chosen direction. Height is a function of s, and s is a function of time. Nesting functions this way creates a composite function.

On the same hill, stopping the vehicle stops the height change. Moving forward faster changes height faster; reversing flips the sign. The vertical rate combines two factors: how much height changes per meter along the track, and how many track meters are traveled per second. Their product is height change per second. This is the chain rule. Because the first ratio uses actual path length, its magnitude cannot exceed one; it is not the usual rise divided by horizontal run.

Suppose we want the rate at which a vehicle on a track gains height. First look at how height changes over a short distance along the track. That is the local slope. Multiply the slope by speed along the track to obtain the height change per unit time. Reversing the vehicle on the same slope reverses the vertical change. Height per distance times distance per time cancels the intermediate distance. The units give a check on the chain rule.

For sine of three x, differentiate the outer sine to get cosine of three x, then multiply by the derivative of the inner three x, which is three. For sine of x squared, the outer derivative is cosine of x squared and the inner derivative is two x. Multiply them. We are differentiating with respect to x here. If x itself changes with time, a time derivative needs another factor, d x over d t.

If the exponent is cosine x plus three x, the outer exponential remains unchanged and is multiplied by the inner derivative, minus sine x plus three. Finally, suppose the exponent is sine of three x plus sine of x squared. Add the two inner derivatives we just found: three cosine of three x plus two x cosine of x squared. Multiply that sum by the original exponential. Longer expressions still follow the same rule: preserve the outer input and include every inner rate.

We can now read the opening motion more precisely. Position differences between two times give average velocity, while motion at a particular instant comes from the tangent to the position graph. Differentiation turns that slope into a function. Power rules handled simple movement, trigonometric functions connected to rotation and oscillation, and the chain rule handled nested relationships such as a hilly track. The next question is how quickly velocity itself changes. That leads to acceleration, and then to integration, which accumulates motion back into position.
