In BeamNG.drive, a car hits rocks, crumples, and tumbles in a different direction. How can we connect the motion before and after contact? We will begin with the momentum change produced by force, then add center of mass and two-object contact conditions. To understand the complex collision, first calculate momentum for one object moving along one direction.

You arrive after a box starts moving. You cannot tell whether it received a large short push or a smaller long one, but can measure mass and velocity. Starting from rest under constant force, velocity is acceleration times time. Substitute force divided by mass, and mass times velocity equals force times time.

Momentum equals mass times velocity and points along the velocity. Multiply mass in kilograms by velocity in metres per second. A newton multiplied by a second gives the same unit. It depends on current state. A slowly rolling pencil and a heavy rolling car require different momentum changes to stop. At equal velocity, twice the mass means twice the momentum; direction matters too.

Force times duration gives momentum change. With varying force, add force times tiny time intervals: the time integral of force, called impulse. Final momentum equals its initial value plus this integral. We accumulate signed vector changes in momentum, not force magnitude itself.

Impulse records both the strength of a force and how long it acts. Hold force fixed and extend its duration. The shaded area under the force-time graph grows and momentum changes by that amount. Divide the momentum change by mass to get the velocity change. Attach it to the original velocity to find the final one.

Differentiate the integral relation: net force is the rate of momentum change. The same change over less time requires greater average force. With constant mass, differentiating momentum gives mass times acceleration, connecting back to Newton’s second law.

A person pushing against the ground to start a box transfers opposite momentum to Earth. Its huge mass makes the velocity change tiny. A tree impact or friction in grass transfers the box’s momentum back to the ground. Another box can receive it instead. Momentum moves within the system; define which bodies the system includes.

Internal bodies can move while total momentum stays constant when net external force is zero. Internal force pairs cancel in the system sum. One body stopping does not mean momentum vanished. For a short collision we often neglect external impulse and conserve the pair’s total momentum.

Center of mass is the representative position of distributed mass. In nearly uniform gravity it coincides with center of gravity. Suspend an irregular plate and let it settle: its center lies along the vertical through the suspension point. Repeat at another point; the lines intersect. A third suspension checks the same intersection.

Locating the centre of mass takes account of both position and the mass at each point. Place weights at opposite ends of a bar and increase just one mass. The centre marker shifts towards the heavier side. The mass-weighted sum of positions divided by total mass gives that location.

A tossed hammer’s tip traces a complex path, while its center of mass near the head follows a parabola under uniform gravity. Separate translation of that point from rotation around it. An irregular plate with a marked center demonstrates the same relationship. Exclude the initial held frame: it has not begun free flight.

Physics is simpler with its origin at the center of mass, but an artist may place the model origin at a convenient tip. Distinguish world center-of-mass position from rendering origin. Rotate the body-space offset by current orientation to connect the two representations.

A rigid body has a fixed body-space center of mass; a system with moving parts does not. Redistributing people to one side of Earth shifts the center relative to its geometric center, while internal redistribution alone does not change total center-of-mass motion under unchanged external force. A constrained fan may vibrate from imbalance. Free bodies can also tumble; orientation behavior requires their inertia distribution.

Earth and Moon have a shared center of mass. Earth’s greater mass places it inside Earth, though not at its geometric center. A simplified solar orbit uses this common center, neglecting perturbations from other bodies. With the system and conserved momentum defined, we can calculate collision outcomes.

A projectile strikes the castle wall. Pieces of the wall that had been stationary start moving immediately after impact. We now need to include the struck object as well as the projectile when finding the state after contact. Approximate a brief collision as an instantaneous momentum change. Prime marks post-impact velocities. Sum mass times velocity for both bodies before and after; the totals match. But one equation cannot uniquely determine two new velocities. We need an additional condition, such as sticking or rebound behavior.

When a projectile embeds in a block they share one velocity after impact. The block starts at rest, so the incoming momentum comes from the projectile. If external impulse is negligible during the short impact, divide that momentum by their combined mass. A fast light projectile can leave the attached pair moving slowly.

Apply the same reasoning to two cars approaching from different directions. The first travels west and the other northwest. Take east and north as positive and follow the two velocity arrows. If they stick, we want the direction and speed of the pair.

Multiply each velocity by its mass and add the momentum arrows head to tail. Add horizontal components together and vertical components together. The resultant points between the incoming directions. Divide by total mass for the velocity of the joined cars. Averaging their speed magnitudes would discard both masses and directions.

Compute impulse instead of resolving every force during impact. Apply equal and opposite impulses to the bodies. In this frictionless model, correct approach along the contact normal, leaving tangential motion unchanged. Decomposing incoming motion into inward and sideways parts reveals what needs changing.

Subtract body two’s velocity from body one’s for relative velocity. Dot it with the unit normal to get the normal component. Under our convention, positive means approaching. Do not add a fresh restitution impulse to separating contacts. Zeroing the whole relative velocity would incorrectly remove sideways motion.

Restitution distinguishes a beanbag from a bouncy ball. It sets post-impact normal relative speed as a fraction of the incoming value. Zero removes normal relative speed; one reverses it with equal magnitude. In this simple collision, perfect elasticity also conserves kinetic energy. Visible rebound alone does not establish perfect elasticity.

How much a ball rebounds depends on its incoming velocity along the contact normal. Drop it from the same height and raise only restitution. Its outgoing vertical arrow grows longer. At fixed gravity the apex height is proportional to launch speed squared, so the rebound height ratio is restitution squared. On a slope the tangential velocity can remain even when the normal velocity vanishes.

Apply minus k n impulse to body one and plus k n to body two. Divide each impulse by its body’s mass to obtain velocity change. A lighter body responds more strongly to equal impulse. The scalar k is the impulse magnitude only when n is a unit vector.

Subtract the new velocities and dot with the normal. Require the result to equal minus restitution times the incoming normal component. Collect terms containing k: the numerator is one plus restitution times the incoming normal dot product; the denominator is the sum of inverse masses times normal length squared. A unit normal makes the last factor one.

For a surface that does not respond to force, use zero inverse mass rather than a huge numerical mass. Its velocity receives no impulse-driven change. When only one body responds, its mass cancels from the velocity-change formula, giving the same result for equal incoming velocity and restitution.

Which way should an impulse point on a sloping wall? Attach a normal arrow at contact and project the relative velocity onto that axis. Here the normal deliberately has non-unit length. Its squared length enters the formula so merely drawing a longer normal does not increase the physical impulse.

Check which part of the velocity arrow the impulse changes. Dividing impulse by mass gives the velocity change. Add it to the incoming velocity to obtain the outgoing arrow. The incoming normal part turns outward. In this frictionless model the tangential part stays the same, so the ball still travels sideways.

Lower only the restitution in the same collision. As restitution approaches zero the outgoing normal speed shrinks. At one it rebounds with the incoming normal speed. The surviving sideways arrow shows exactly which component restitution affects.

A real beanbag can lose sideways speed through friction. Our model preserves tangential velocity because it omits friction. Changing it requires a tangential impulse. If both bodies move, both masses matter; if they rotate, contact-point rotational velocity matters too. The observed result reveals the model’s limits.

Detection may find bodies already overlapping. Preventing further approach does not instantly remove existing overlap. Strategies include finding the contact time or allowing small penetration and correcting position or separation velocity. Many contacts and floating-point error make exact-touching treatment more complicated.

Zero inverse mass does not always mean stationary. A kinematic platform can follow a prescribed path without responding to force. Its velocity still enters the relative velocity of a colliding ball. Inverse mass controls impulse response; velocity describes how the contact surface moves.

To change a box's velocity once in game code, the required impulse is mass times velocity change. Apply that impulse once. To achieve the same effect with force, apply it over time until its area equals the impulse. Reapplying the impulse every frame adds the velocity change each time. The motion distinguishes a single impulse from a force accumulated over time.

We approximate an impulse as instantaneous while retaining the force-integral relationship. Dirac delta represents that idea. Take a rectangle of width w and height one over w. Shrink its width while keeping unit area. Height grows but total area stays one. Delta is a distribution interpreted through integration and limits, not an ordinary function evaluated as infinity at zero.
