In Satisfactory, adjusting a rail endpoint and joining it to the next section creates a continuing curved route. If we design a long path with one polynomial, editing one part can affect distant parts. Connecting smaller curves lets us work on the pieces. We will begin with those joins, find conditions for both shape and motion to continue smoothly, and then generate tangents automatically.

One option is degree elevation. In power form, adding a zero higher-order coefficient preserves the curve. In Bézier form, keep the endpoints and blend neighboring controls at prescribed ratios to obtain new ones. Elevation itself preserves the shape while adding controls for later editing. Symmetric Hermite constraints naturally use odd degrees: linear for endpoint positions, cubic when adding first derivatives, and quintic when adding second derivatives.

Elevating a cubic to a quartic blends neighboring controls with one quarter and three quarters, then halves, then three quarters and one quarter. Repeated elevation makes evaluation and editing more complex, while influence remains global. High-degree interpolation can also oscillate between samples. This motivates splines made from lower-degree pieces.

Walking several pieces as one path first requires locating the current piece. Assign equal parameter intervals to the pieces. The integer part selects the segment and the fractional part locates the point within it. On the next segment local progress starts at zero again, while joined endpoints should occupy the same position.

For animation, map physical time to the global parameter first. Then select a segment and local progress, and evaluate that curve. Changing the time mapping changes motion along the same route. Advancing the parameter uniformly does not guarantee equal distances in space.

Call the shared endpoints knots in this interpolating construction. The previous segment must end where the next begins. With n segments there are n plus one such points. A knot k is one position; a segment function q takes local progress and returns a position.

Each knot has an incoming derivative and an outgoing derivative: how the preceding segment arrives and how the following segment departs. Store the position and these vectors to evaluate Hermite pieces. For direct graphical editing, the same information can be displayed as Bézier handles.

Name the incoming handle f and outgoing handle a. The incoming derivative is three times the vector from f to k, and the outgoing derivative is three times the vector from k to a. Handle distances therefore equal one third of the derivative magnitude. Storing connected cubic controls in one sequence places shared knots at indices zero, three, six, and so on.

In Super Mario Maker 2, a right-angle track corner can be changed into a rounded section. We can see why connecting positions and joining travel directions smoothly are different requirements. What must agree for a smooth join? Matching both direction and magnitude connects the derivatives. If directions agree but lengths differ, the shape may look smooth while motion jumps in speed under a uniform parameter clock. Setting both derivatives to zero can instead stop at a corner before departing in another direction.

Parametric continuity distinguishes these cases. C zero joins positions. C one also joins first derivatives. C two additionally joins second derivatives. When the parameter is physical time, these correspond to position, velocity, and acceleration. Higher-order continuity includes all lower-order requirements.

C one does not mean constant velocity. Velocity may change, but without an instantaneous jump over intermediate values. A physical mechanism cannot make such a jump with finite acceleration; an animation may look jerky. Inside each polynomial piece, derivatives remain continuous, so our main concern is the joins.

For cubic segments with equal parameter intervals, C one is straightforward: match the incoming and outgoing derivatives. In handle form, k minus f equals a minus k. Rearranging makes k the midpoint of the two handles. The handles lie on opposite sides of the knot at equal distances along one line.

C two involves one more neighboring control. Equate the preceding ending acceleration and the following starting acceleration, then cancel the common factor six. This requires the two extended points shown to coincide. Extend each next-to-adjacent control-polygon edge once beyond its handle. If their ends meet, the existing C-one join is also C two.

What if the left segment takes two seconds and the right takes one? Equal handles no longer match physical velocity. Divide each local derivative by its segment duration. Here the left handle must be twice the right to match actual velocity at the join. Acceleration scales by the square of duration.

Geometric continuity describes the shape. For regular G-one joins, nonzero tangents point in the same forward direction; their magnitudes may differ. A time reparameterization can compensate for the speed difference by changing its derivative. We adjust travel speed, not jump the position mapping.

G two also matches bending. In three dimensions, equal curvature magnitudes alone are insufficient: the curvature vectors, including the bending direction, must agree. A path turning sideways and then upward is not a G-two join merely because both bends have the same radius.

Every additional continuity condition consumes freedom. Fix one cubic, then matching the next position fixes its first control, matching velocity fixes the second, and matching acceleration fixes the third. Matching the third derivative fixes the fourth as well: it is the continuation of the same polynomial. C two is the highest generic continuity that leaves cubic pieces free to differ.

A Launch Star in Super Mario Galaxy sends Mario along a curved flight toward the next planet. Let us build this kind of motion through several points in our own path. We know how to join the pieces, but adjusting every tangent by hand can be tedious. Can we specify only the knots and calculate the tangents automatically? The following methods choose the derivatives for Hermite pieces rather than creating a new evaluation formula.

Use the vector from the previous knot to the next as the middle tangent direction. Giving both incoming and outgoing tangents half that vector produces the uniform Catmull–Rom construction used here. Algebraically, this is the average of the incoming and outgoing control-polygon edges.

Use the neighbours to construct a tangent at the middle knot. Draw an arrow from the previous to the next point and attach half of it at the middle knot. This is the Catmull–Rom tangent for this uniform spacing. The arriving and departing pieces share the tangent, joining both direction and parametric velocity.

You may want the automatic curve tighter or biased toward one side. Kochanek–Bartels, or TCB, splines offer three controls: tension, continuity, and bias. Setting all three to zero returns the uniform Catmull–Rom construction just used.

Change tension alone first. It multiplies the baseline tangents by one minus tension. At one, both become zero, giving straight-shaped pieces that stop at every knot. At minus one the tangents double. Values above one reverse the tangents and can introduce loops.

Next is the continuity control. At zero, incoming and outgoing tangents match, giving C one. Set it to minus one with the other controls zero, and each segment becomes a constant-speed straight line to the next knot. Its shape matches the tension-one case, but its motion does not.

Continuity changes the edge weights in opposite ways. The incoming tangent uses half of one minus C for the preceding edge and half of one plus C for the next. The outgoing tangent swaps those weights. The two tangents can therefore differ and create a corner.

Bias tilts both tangents toward one side together. The preceding edge gets half of one plus B, and the following edge half of one minus B. Positive bias follows the preceding direction more; negative bias anticipates the following direction. The curve visits the same knots with a different turning behavior.

Combine the controls by multiplying the tension, continuity, and bias factors into each edge weight. The formula looks long, but it combines the three roles we just observed. Values need not be identical at every knot; adjust the knot near a particular corner when local control is needed.

Endpoints lack one neighbor, so they need a policy. Set the derivatives to zero when the motion should begin and end at rest, or add phantom points used only for tangent calculation. Since the first tangent is half the vector from the phantom to the next point, a zero tangent requires copying the next point—not the start point.

Users can place phantom points to control departure. Another choice fits a quadratic through the first three knots and uses its endpoint derivative. With unit parameter spacing, the first derivative is minus three k zero plus four k one minus k two, divided by two. Select the boundary rule to match the intended start and finish. Cardinal spline usually names a family of interpolating curves with a common tangent-scale control, not this one endpoint policy.

Now connect the calculations. For Lagrange nodes zero, one, and two, the first basis is t minus one times t minus two divided by two. The middle is minus t times t minus two. The last is t times t minus one divided by two. Substitute each node to check that only its own basis equals one.

Consider a projectile starting at the origin. It reaches its maximum height after one second and lands at the chosen forward distance after two seconds. Call the maximum height h and the total forward distance d. With no sideways movement, x is zero. Constant forward speed gives z equal to d t over two. Height equals initial vertical velocity times time plus one half the vertical acceleration times time squared. With the peak at one second and a return to ground at two, the expression factors as y = h t (2 − t), where t is the numerical time in seconds. It gives h at one and zero at two.

Now locate the current position on this Bézier curve. Fix the progress marker and locate points in that same ratio between neighbouring controls. Repeat the interpolation between the new points. The final single point is the current curve position. Move the marker and all intermediate points move, making the last point trace the curve.

Rewriting this curve in Hermite form must preserve its path. Keep the two endpoints and form their derivatives from the offsets to the adjacent handles. In monomial form collect the same position function by powers of the parameter. Its inputs look different but the same progress must give the same point.

Place the same progress marker on both representations to check agreement. First compare the two positions, then the tangent arrows attached there. Differentiating each coordinate gives the tangent components. Here it heads left and slightly down. With the same parameter both representations agree in direction and magnitude.

The fourth check sums the quadratic Bernstein weights. Expand the first into one minus two t plus t squared. Add two t minus two t squared, then t squared. Both the linear and quadratic terms cancel, leaving one—the partition-of-unity condition used for the convex hull property.

For the next checks, choose controls from the desired motion. Keep every control at one point for a constant curve. Space them equally from start to end for a constant-speed line. For a smooth start and stop along that line, cubic controls are start, start, end, end. Handle placement changes timing even when the line is unchanged.

The eighth case alternates controls start, end, start, end. It may look as though the particle should reverse, but it moves monotonically toward the end. Its blend derivative is three times the square of the quantity two t minus one, so it is nonnegative. It stops only at the midpoint, then accelerates in the same direction.

The ninth problem represents the earlier projectile using a cubic Bézier tool. Map its two seconds to u from zero to one. Height becomes four h u times one minus u, and forward position d u. The cubic coefficient is zero. Conversion gives both internal handles height four h over three, with forward positions d over three and two d over three.

Transfer a known parabola to handles in a graphics editor. Select the piece's endpoints on the original parabola. Express horizontal position through new progress, then insert that position into the original height function. Both coordinates now share one progress parameter. Converting those polynomials to Bézier form gives the control points for the editor.

Connect the controls and overlay the result on the original parabola. The first and last marks lie on the curve. The inner marks are handles setting the endpoint directions, rather than samples to pass through. The control polygon looks different from the curve, yet the transferred piece lies on the original parabola.

Finally extract only the interval between two marks on this curve. Map new progress zero to the left mark and one to the right mark. Read both endpoint positions from the original curve and scale their derivatives by the interval width to obtain handles for the new parameter.

Now split the same curve halfway and keep both pieces. Collect the de Casteljau midpoints and the left piece ends exactly where the right begins. The differently coloured pieces still trace the original path. To preserve the motion as each runs from zero to one, match their durations as well.

Degree elevation uses one more control point while preserving the curve. Fix the endpoints and place the new interior controls between neighbouring original controls. Two control polygons appear but their curves coincide. The number of handles in an editor and the shape traced by the object are different properties.

Now watch the train following the completed rails through the bends of a long route. We can say which quantities to check when building motion like this ourselves. A long path did not require one enormous formula. It required manageable pieces and explicit rules for joining their positions, directions, and rates of change. The needed continuity depends on whether smooth shape is enough or velocity and acceleration must also connect. With those criteria, manual handles and automatic tangents become understandable design tools.
