When a car takes a bend in Forza Horizon 5, both its speedometer reading and its travel direction change. Would changing only direction still produce acceleration if speed stayed constant? We will simplify the path to a circle, relate speed to radius, then extend the picture to a three-dimensional rotation axis. Finally, worked problems connect units and calculus. First, model one point moving around a circle at constant speed.

Track one point on the rotating object, ignoring its size and spin for now. Model it as a particle on a circle. Call the radius r and its speed along the circle s. Speed is the length of the velocity vector, whose direction is tangent to the circle at every point. That tangent is perpendicular to the radius.

Take two nearby positions. Their velocity arrows have equal lengths but different directions. Translate both arrows to a common tail, then draw the change from the earlier arrow tip to the later tip. That is delta velocity. The triangle formed by the two radii and the one formed by the velocity vectors have the same included angle, so they are similar.

Similarity gives the magnitude of delta velocity over speed equal to the magnitude of delta position over radius. The straight line between positions is a chord; the traveled distance is an arc. For a finite interval the chord is shorter, so they are not equal. As the interval approaches zero, chord length divided by elapsed time approaches instantaneous speed. That limit makes the acceleration result exact.

Acceleration is the limit of velocity change divided by elapsed time. The expression becomes speed over radius times speed: speed squared over radius. The velocity difference points inward at the midpoint of the nearby positions, and in the limit it points exactly toward the center. This inward acceleration is centripetal acceleration. Constant speed still permits acceleration because velocity direction changes.

At ten meters per second on a five-meter radius, centripetal acceleration is twenty meters per second squared. Double speed at the same radius and acceleration quadruples. Double radius at the same speed and it halves, because the turn is gentler. Centripetal acceleration does not name a new physical force. The net effect of forces such as tire friction or string tension produces the required inward acceleration.

Circular position can also be described by an angle. At angle theta, x is r cosine theta and y is r sine theta. For constant angular velocity omega, theta equals the initial angle plus omega t. Omega is measured in radians per second, with its sign distinguishing rotation direction. Speed is nonnegative, so its relationship to omega uses the magnitude of omega.

Differentiate the position with respect to time. Cosine becomes minus sine and sine becomes cosine, while the chain rule contributes omega from the changing angle. Differentiate again and both components contain minus r omega squared times their original cosine or sine. Acceleration is therefore minus omega squared times the radius vector: inward, with magnitude r omega squared. This matches the geometric derivation.

One revolution covers two pi r, so its period is two pi r divided by speed. Frequency, revolutions per second, is the reciprocal. Multiply frequency by two pi radians per revolution to obtain angular-speed magnitude. Period is in seconds, frequency in hertz, and angular speed in radians per second. They describe the same motion but are different quantities.

A point on Earth’s equator can be modeled as circling the rotation axis. Using the illustrative approximations six thousand three hundred seventy-one kilometers and a twenty-four-hour revolution gives centripetal acceleration of about zero point zero three three seven meters per second squared. Near the poles, the perpendicular distance to the axis decreases. Circular radius is distance to the rotation axis, not automatically Earth’s full radius at every latitude.

Feeling pushed outward in a turning car does not contradict inward acceleration. In a ground-fixed frame, inertia carries the body straight until the car or seat supplies an inward force. In a frame rotating with the car, an outward centrifugal inertial force can be introduced. Do not add both arrows as though they were independent physical forces without specifying the reference frame.

In Tears of the Kingdom, Ultrahand lifts a sail, turns its mast upright, and attaches it to a raft. The chosen rotation axis changes the object’s orientation. In a plane, direction can be described as clockwise or counterclockwise. In three dimensions we also need the rotation axis. Multiply a unit axis vector n by angular speed with the chosen sign to form angular velocity. This vector points along the rotation axis, not along the particle’s motion. Its cross product with the radius gives tangential velocity.

Cross-product direction depends on order and coordinate convention. This diagram uses a right-handed frame: x right, y up, and z out of the screen. Angular velocity along positive z crossed with a radius along positive x gives velocity along positive y. Reversing the cross-product order reverses the result. An engine using a left-handed frame or a different axis layout must display the corresponding axis and positive-rotation convention consistently.

Cross-product magnitude is the product of vector lengths and the sine of their angle. For a circular radius perpendicular to the axis, the sine is one, leaving angular speed times radius, exactly our speed relation. A position component parallel to the axis contributes nothing to the cross product. The effective circular radius is the component perpendicular to the rotation axis.

Conversely, given a point’s radius and velocity in genuine circular motion, divide radius crossed with velocity by radius length squared. The radius must be nonzero, and the order is now radius followed by velocity. Radius two and speed six give angular speed three. Doubling both radius and speed quadruples both numerator and denominator, leaving the same angular velocity.

One point’s velocity cannot determine every possible angular-velocity component. Adding rotation around an axis parallel to that point’s radius does not change its velocity. The formula recovers only the angular-velocity component perpendicular to the radius. A radial component of linear velocity also disappears in the cross product. Applied to arbitrary motion, the expression extracts the tangential change seen about the chosen reference point.

Observe a constant-velocity object passing a fixed point off its straight path. Far away its viewing direction changes slowly; nearby that direction sweeps quickly. Constant linear velocity therefore need not mean constant angular velocity about an arbitrary point. For this straight pass, the magnitude peaks at closest approach and then decreases. Constant angular velocity in circular motion referred specifically to the circle’s center.

On a windmill in Besiege, the portion near the hub and the blade tip turn together. Their distances from the hub determine how far each travels around a turn. For points on one rotating rigid body, choose each circular center on the rotation axis. Points farther from the axis move faster and nearer points slower, yet all share the same angular velocity. Their linear velocities follow from angular velocity crossed with radius. The structure of a position vector crossed with another vector will return in torque calculations for rigid-body motion. First, check the present tools through worked problems.

The first problem converts pascals to psi. A pascal is one newton per square meter, while psi is one pound-force per square inch. Convert both force and area. One pound-force is about four point four four eight two newtons, and one inch is zero point zero two five four meters. Because area is squared, the length conversion must also be squared. One psi is therefore about six thousand eight hundred ninety-four point seven five seven pascals.

The question asks for psi per pascal, so take the reciprocal: about zero point zero zero zero one four five zero three eight psi. Converting only force, or using the length conversion without squaring it, gives the wrong dimensions. Write the quantities and keep their units inside the calculation to catch those errors.

Problems two through seven follow one particle. From zero to just before two seconds, position is two t minus t squared. From two to just before four it stays at zero. From four to just before seven it oscillates as sine of pi t. From seven onward it follows seven minus t in the negative direction. Select the active time interval first, then apply its formula.

The first segment starts at zero, reaches position one at one second, and returns to zero at two. It then rests for two seconds. The sine segment reaches one at four point five, minus one at five point five, and one again at six point five. At seven it returns to zero, then reaches minus one at eight and minus two at nine. Position is continuous at the joins, but slopes jump; continuity of position does not guarantee a defined velocity.

Average velocity is final position minus initial position divided by elapsed time. From zero to one second, position changes from zero to one, giving one. From one to two it returns from one to zero, giving minus one. Across the entire zero-to-two interval, displacement is zero and average velocity is zero, despite nonzero distance traveled.

From five point five to six point five seconds, the sine segment goes from minus one to plus one in one second, so average velocity is two. Over zero to nine seconds, the endpoints are zero and minus two, giving minus two ninths. Intermediate reversals do not change how average velocity is calculated from endpoint positions and elapsed time.

Differentiate each position segment. Two t minus t squared becomes two minus two t; constant zero becomes zero. Sine of pi t becomes pi cosine of pi t by the chain rule. Seven minus t becomes minus one. At two, four, and seven seconds, the left and right slopes differ, so the ordinary derivative is not defined at those joins.

At zero point one seconds, velocity is two minus zero point two, or one point eight. At one second it is zero, and at one point nine it is two minus three point eight, or minus one point eight. These correspond to rising, turning, and falling on the first position curve. A positive position can coexist with negative velocity, as the returning particle shows.

At four point one seconds, evaluate pi cosine of four point one pi. Periodicity reduces it to pi cosine of zero point one pi, about two point nine eight eight. At five seconds, cosine of five pi is minus one, giving minus pi. At six point five, cosine is zero, so velocity is zero. At eight and nine, the final linear segment has constant velocity minus one.

Differentiate velocity once more. Two minus two t gives minus two, and constant zero gives zero. Pi cosine of pi t becomes minus pi squared sine of pi t: the cosine derivative supplies the minus sign and the inner derivative supplies another pi. Constant minus one gives zero. Again, calculate only within segments, excluding the joins.

At zero point one, one, and one point nine seconds, acceleration is minus two throughout the first segment. At four point one it is minus pi squared times sine of zero point one pi, about minus three point zero five. At five, sine is zero and acceleration is zero; at six point five, sine is one and acceleration is minus pi squared. At eight and nine, constant velocity gives zero acceleration. Zero velocity and zero acceleration need not occur together.

The next projectile starts at zero feet horizontally and ten feet vertically, with speed one hundred fifty feet per second at forty degrees above horizontal. Choose upward positive and gravity magnitude thirty-two feet per second squared. Horizontal velocity is one hundred fifty cosine forty degrees, about one hundred fourteen point nine one; vertical velocity is one hundred fifty sine forty degrees, about ninety-six point four two. Match the calculator’s degree or radian setting to the stated angle.

Apex time is initial vertical velocity divided by gravity, about three point zero one three seconds. Multiplying by horizontal velocity gives an x coordinate of about three hundred forty-six point two two feet. For y, add the rise to the initial ten-foot height. Initial vertical velocity squared over sixty-four gives a rise of about one hundred forty-five point two six feet, so the apex is about one hundred fifty-five point two six feet high.

The requested return height is the launch height of ten feet, not ground level. The return time is twice the apex time, about six point zero two six seconds. Multiplying by horizontal velocity gives a horizontal displacement of about six hundred ninety-two point four four feet. A ground-impact question would require a different quadratic including the initial height. Here the same-height condition permits symmetry.

Return to arbitrary acceleration. Dot both sides of displacement equals initial velocity times time plus one half acceleration times time squared with the acceleration vector. The result is scalar. Let A be acceleration length squared, B the dot product of initial velocity with acceleration, and D the dot product of displacement with acceleration. Then one half A t squared plus B t minus D equals zero.

The quadratic formula gives minus B plus or minus the square root of B squared plus two A D, divided by A. Acceleration must be nonzero. These are candidate times satisfying the projected coordinate. Substitute each into the original vector position equation to check every component. The result agrees with projecting onto a unit acceleration direction, but the intermediate coefficient units differ because this dot product used an unnormalized vector.

The imaginary unit i satisfies i squared equals minus one. Substitute i x into the exponential series: one plus i x plus i x squared over two factorial, and so on. Powers of i cycle through one, i, minus one, and minus i. Even powers therefore contribute real terms, while odd powers contribute terms carrying i.

The real terms form one minus x squared over two factorial plus x to the fourth over four factorial: the cosine series. The coefficients of i form the sine series. Thus e to the i x equals cosine x plus i sine x, Euler’s formula. On the complex plane this is a point on the unit circle, connecting the exponential function with rotation.

The final problem approximates a space-station orbit as a circle, using the stipulated altitude of three hundred forty kilometers and speed of twenty-seven thousand seven hundred forty kilometers per hour. These are exercise inputs, not current operational data. The orbital radius is measured from Earth’s center, so add altitude to the assumed Earth radius of six thousand three hundred seventy-one kilometers. This gives six million seven hundred eleven thousand meters. Speed converts to about seven thousand seven hundred five point five six meters per second.

Divide circumference, two pi r, by speed to obtain about five thousand four hundred seventy-two seconds, or ninety-one point two minutes. Speed squared divided by radius gives centripetal acceleration about eight point eight five meters per second squared. That is not tiny compared with surface gravity. Floating in orbit is explained by the station and occupants falling together, not by an absence of gravity. Correct radius and units are essential.

The opening car changed travel direction. Simplifying this to constant-speed circular motion made the velocity vector’s derivative point inward. Similar triangles and differentiation gave the same acceleration, and angular velocity with cross products extended it to three dimensions. The problems connected units, piecewise position, derivatives, accumulation, and arrival times. Before choosing a formula, identify the object, reference point, time, and units. Then the equations and visible motion can describe the same event.
