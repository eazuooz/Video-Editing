In Super Mario Odyssey, Mario jumps and lands: his whole body moves while his arms and legs change pose. How can a computer coordinate these different parts of one character? We will follow skinning, which changes the large shape, then normal mapping, which uses surface directions to shade fine detail. First, let us distinguish moving the whole character from bending its joints.

One matrix moves and orients an entire character like a statue. An elbow needs connected parts such as torso, upper arm, and forearm to move relative to each other. A hierarchy of rigid parts can animate a robot, but each part remains rigid and does not produce continuous deformation through skin or clothing.

When a bicycle jumps in Riders Republic, its frame retains its shape while the rider bends and extends arms and knees. To build this distinction ourselves, we will attach a small coordinate frame to each joint. Skeletal animation replaces the part hierarchy with virtual bones. A bone is a moving coordinate frame, not the stick used to visualize it. Mesh vertices are associated with bones and respond to their transforms. A single influence behaves rigidly; multiple influences blend their effects.

Skinning assigns which bones affect each vertex and by how much. Those proportions are skin weights. An elbow vertex may be influenced by both arm bones. Rigging can also include animator controls beyond these assignments. Automatic weights are a starting point, but extreme poses still need inspection.

When an elbow bends, where should a point on the sleeve go? Blue marks where the upper arm alone would put the vertex; green marks the forearm's proposal. The sleeve vertex lies between them, blended according to each bone's influence. This is linear blend skinning. We blend the transformed positions, rather than the angles.

Which bone has more influence on this vertex? Hold the pose still and gradually increase the forearm's share. As the forearm gains influence, the upper arm loses it and the sleeve point approaches green. Varying that blend across neighboring vertices gives the sleeve a continuous bend.

A vertex stores influencing bone indices and weights. In the simple method, it also stores a position in each influencing bone's space. Nonnegative weights sum to one so identical proposed positions remain unchanged. Four influences may be an implementation limit, but it is not a mathematical maximum.

Begin by zeroing one vertex's output. For each influencing bone, fetch its current matrix, transform the bone-space position into model space, multiply by the weight, and accumulate. Then proceed to the next vertex. The loop repeats the two-proposal diagram across the mesh.

Unchanged normals would make lighting fail to follow the deformation. Transform their directional components without translation, blend them, and renormalize. This common simple treatment is an approximation used with rotation-focused skinning. Nonuniform scale or shear requires separate normal-transformation handling, including inverse-transpose considerations.

The bone-space positions come from a reference configuration called the bind pose: the original mesh and skeleton arrangement used for binding. Visualized bones are not physical rods pushing the mesh. Knowing each vertex and bone frame in the bind pose lets us express the vertex in that bone's coordinates.

Where is a bind-pose vertex in the current pose? The inverse bind matrix expresses the point relative to its bone. The current bone matrix then places the point in model space in the new pose. Always start from the bind position so last frame's motion is not applied twice.

A button on the sleeve makes those two steps easier to see. Draw a line from the original elbow to the button. It locates the button relative to the bone. Move the elbow: the line follows it, and its tip is the button's new position. Without removing the original elbow placement, we would count that offset twice.

We can reduce storage by keeping one bind-pose position per vertex instead of one per bone. Precombine each bone's inverse bind transform with its current transform. For a vertex, blend the required matrices using its weights, then apply that blended matrix once.

The equivalence follows from distributivity: transform the same input with several matrices and sum, or sum the weighted matrices first and transform once. This can reuse work across attributes. However, a linear blend of rotation matrices is not generally another pure rotation, so joints can collapse or lose volume during twisting.

Skinning therefore involves more than creating bones. Test joint extremes and adjust the weights and mesh. Muscle bulging or flesh spreading under load may require additional deformation methods. We have changed vertices to shape the large form; next we will represent small surface details without adding a vertex for every bump.

As a flashlight sweeps over a metal wall in Doom 3, small grooves emerge through changes in brightness. We will now compare one way to create this kind of surface detail on a fixed surface in our own implementation. Textures can store more than color. Bump mapping represents small relief through changes in shading direction. A height map stores height and derives normals from its slope; a normal map stores the directions directly. We will focus on normal maps, which alone do not displace vertices or change the silhouette.

What we need from a normal map is the surface direction, not its color. Red carries the first tangent component, green the second, and blue the outward component. A direction can be negative; an ordinary color channel cannot. Encoding and decoding convert between those ranges. At an untilted point the outward component dominates, giving the map its familiar blue tint.

These colors package direction data rather than decorative color. Altering brightness changes the encoded direction. Import them as normal data, not ordinary sRGB color, and handle renormalization after sampling or compression. A common workflow bakes surface directions from detailed geometry into a texture for a lower-resolution mesh.

Reusing a texture saves memory and authoring work, but normals are directions. If the same values on a box's top and bottom are interpreted as object-space upward directions, both surfaces would point upward. Each patch needs a local frame telling us what outward means there.

Tangent space supplies the local frame: normal N points outward, tangent T follows increasing texture u, and bitangent B follows increasing v. Some references call B the binormal; we will consistently use bitangent. Together they form the TBN basis. Image-up and increasing-v conventions must agree across tools.

At the same surface point, what happens to the normal when only red changes? The formula decodes the stored channels and normalizes the resulting arrow. Lower red and the arrow leans against the tangent; illumination changes under the same light. Eight-bit channels have finite steps, so decoding may introduce a small direction error.

Which way does that arrow point on the model itself? A negative first component points toward decreasing surface U, not toward screen left. Combine the three components along the tangent, bitangent and surface normal to obtain a model-space normal. Rotate the face. Its axes and the resulting arrow turn together, keeping the detail attached.

Across a curved surface, N, T, and B change with position. Even on a flat surface, distorted UV mapping can alter the u and v directions, which need not be perpendicular. Vertex frames can be interpolated across the surface and then adjusted into an orthonormal frame for the intended shading convention.

Either transform the mapped normal into object space or transform lighting and viewing vectors into tangent space. Compared vectors must share a coordinate system. For an orthonormal basis, inverse equals transpose and dot products with the axes recover coordinates. A skewed basis does not generally have that property.

An orthogonal frame need not store all three axes: a cross product of N and T can reconstruct the third. Mirrored UV mapping may require that axis to reverse, so store a handedness sign that distinguishes the frame's orientation: +1 or -1. Reconstruct B as sign times cross(N,T); formats may store this sign in tangent.w.

To calculate T and B, start with three triangle positions and their UV coordinates. Relative to the first vertex, call the first geometric edge q1 and the second edge q2. Let s1 and s2 be the corresponding changes in u, and t1 and t2 the changes in v. We compare displacement in the surface and texture coordinate systems.

We want the two model-space directions corresponding to horizontal and vertical texture travel. For the first edge, its horizontal and vertical texture contributions add up to the observed model edge. The second edge uses the same two directions. Both model edges and both UV changes are known. Solving those two equations gives the unknown tangent and bitangent.

To separate the tangent from the bitangent, first check whether the UV triangle has area. The denominator is the UV determinant. Its magnitude is twice the triangle area, and its sign records orientation. The first expression eliminates the vertical contribution to isolate T; the second eliminates the horizontal contribution to isolate B. These vectors encode travel per texture unit as well as direction, so they need not have unit length.

Check the formula visually: move a point only to the right on the texture. The matching point on the model follows the tangent. Move the texture point up instead. The model point now follows the bitangent. Normalizing the arrows preserves their directions. What changes when the texture is mirrored?

Do not discard the denominator merely because you will normalize. A negative sign survives normalization. Mirroring the u direction changes the determinant to -1 and requires T to reverse. Normalization changes length, not orientation, so the determinant's sign must be preserved.

If UV vertices collapse onto a line, the determinant is zero and the inverse does not exist. Handle the degenerate case before division, for example by repairing the UVs or using a validated fallback frame. Very small determinants can be unstable, and near-zero normals or accumulated tangents also need protected normalization.

We have a frame for one triangle. To obtain vertex data, accumulate the tangents and bitangents of adjacent compatible triangles, starting the accumulators at zero. These are tangent vectors, not triangle normals. The normal is a separately prepared input used in the following orthogonalization.

Remove the accumulated tangent's component along the unit normal: T'=T-dot(N,T)*N, then normalize. Determine handedness from the sign of dot(cross(N,T'),Baccum). Storing the tangent plus this sign prepares us to reconstruct the frame.

Do not average across every adjacent face indiscriminately. UV or normal discontinuities may require separate frames. A mirrored seam can even share position and UV values while needing opposite tangent directions. Averaging them can cancel the vectors, so split the vertices where necessary to preserve each side's frame.

Once UVs, tangents, and handedness are prepared, runtime work can sample the normal, transform it, and evaluate lighting. Much of the preparation resides in asset processing. The map baker and renderer must agree on the tangent-frame convention, or seams and lighting errors can appear.

Large motion and small detail both depend on transforming values between coordinate frames. Skinning blends bind-pose vertices transformed by bones; normal mapping transforms encoded directions through the surface frame. A bending character needs both its positions and its shading directions to remain consistent.
