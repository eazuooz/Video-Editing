Super Mario 64 Changed 3D Gaming

In a 3D world, choosing where to look was as important as choosing where to move. Super Mario 64 let players change the view, turning the search beyond the screen into play.

Two games from the same era use their cameras differently. This is the original Resident Evil2. The character moves, while the camera stays in its fixed position. The view switches to another predefined camera angle. Super Mario64 also lets the player choose where to look.

The camera follows Mario. The C buttons change its position and direction. The viewpoint is where we see the scene from.

Turn the viewpoint to look around and upward. It helps you find routes and Power Stars. Think about where Mario stands separately from what the camera points toward.

A fixed shot does not let you turn the view to inspect what is off screen. Mario can look in another direction while staying in the same place. Being able to look around before moving is the important change.

C-up enters a close view. The stick now looks around. Press A, B, or a C button other than C-up to return to movement control.

Press C-left or C-right. The camera circles Mario. See the same Mario from another side. When the camera cannot go farther, a buzz signals its limit.

Press C-down to pull the camera back. Mario looks smaller. More surroundings fit in view. Look closely, circle around, and pull back. In Super Mario 64, the player is also the cinematographer.

How Horizon turns artist rules into a forest

Hand-placing every plant and rock has limits in a vast world. Having the GPU place objects by authored rules extends an artist's choices across a vast world.

The starting point is a map. It stores where trees should be dense and where water and paths are located. Plant types and appearance are set by region. Artists adjust the maps and plant assets. Automated placement still has human direction.

Next, build a density map. It determines how much vegetation to place. Exclude areas occupied by roads and water. Combine the conditions into a map for placement.

Now compare candidate points with the map. Check the density condition at each candidate and retain the points that pass. Spacing and point patterns are also controlled. This differs from piling trees at arbitrary positions.

Actual objects are placed at the remaining points. Align them with the terrain’s height and orientation, and add variation in scale or rotation. A point in the map becomes a tuft of grass or a tree on screen.

More placement layers can introduce overlaps. The presentation explains how multiple layers can be coordinated by sharing density ranges. Filling grass, rocks and trees separately is not enough.

In order: artist maps, placement conditions, candidate tests, then actual objects. With the computer handling repetitive placement, artists can refine the forest’s character and the flow of its paths more quickly.

How INSIDE uses the previous frame to smooth the next

A single frame struggles to draw thin lines smoothly. INSIDE reuses information from previous frames to reduce unstable edges in motion.

Scenes with thin lines, as in INSIDE, can have shimmering edges in motion. An image consists of small cells. Each cell contains one color. A cell is a pixel; one complete image is a frame. INSIDE adds information from earlier images to its current image. But simply overlaying the old image can leave ghosts behind moving objects. A thin branch can fall between sample positions, appearing in one image and missing the next. The object can stay the same while its thin edge appears to flicker.

Each frame samples a different location inside the pixel. This is called jitter. One image can capture thin-line information that another image missed. Combining those images carefully can make the edges more stable. Think of sampling the left side of a cell first, then its right side in the next image. Combining those different samples gives a better estimate of the thin edge.

A moving camera or object changes its screen position. Use depth and motion information together. Find where the same point was in the previous frame. How Spider-Man chooses a web attachment point

To attach a web to a building, must you aim at an exact point every time? The system uses movement intent to choose where a web should attach. Simple controls can then deliver a complex swing.

Where Spider-Man’s web attaches changes the depth and path of the swing. Yet the player does not aim at a building point every time. The developers take the intended travel direction and choose a suitable attachment point. First watch the real swing dip and then rise forward. In the model, move only the upper attachment to compare how the arc changes.

An early approach cast multiple lines to find places where they meet buildings. This kind of test is called a raycast. But between those test lines, thin structures and corners could be missed. Adding more rays alone was not a convenient fix. Imagine sending out several narrow flashlight beams. A thin post between the test lines can be missed.

Buildings were marked with simple box volumes. Choosing candidates on continuous faces helps reduce the gaps between test lines. Production tools automate marking the large city. Finally, check whether the result touches real geometry. Now treat a whole building face as the candidate region instead of isolated lines. The box makes candidate selection easier; the real geometry is still checked afterward.

Next, construct an ideal reference. Use stick direction, desired rope length and slope to identify a good starting position. Combine a nearby point on the building with one toward travel. Adjust candidates that would let the player fall too deeply. If you want to move forward, an attachment behind you would pull backward. An attachment that is too low can also send the swing close to the ground. Watch the real line and movement, then compare the model’s higher and lower anchors.

When there are several candidates, score factors such as direction, distance and slope. Choose a well-scoring attachment point. Avoid forcing a connection to a poor location. Debug views of selected points and movement history helped the developers tune the behavior. Each candidate is checked for direction and a suitable line length. Those scores are hidden in gameplay, but the resulting swings do not require aiming at every point.

Once the web is taut, consider gravity and its direction. A good anchor lets you travel in the intended direction. The game chooses the attachment so we can focus on moving. Choosing a suitable attachment in the same city helps one swing lead naturally into the next.

Why low health can make you push forward in DOOM

Hiding and waiting when health is low can interrupt the flow of combat. Attacking at close range creates opportunities to recover health. Confronting danger, rather than hiding, becomes a way to survive.

When health runs low in DOOM, getting closer to an enemy can be the answer. Rather than encouraging hiding and waiting, the developers wanted reasons to engage in combat. Follow the loop from approach and attack to recovery. Waiting behind cover for health would interrupt the flow of combat. The designers placed recovery opportunities near enemies to encourage re-entering the fight.

Attacking can make an enemy stagger, opening a finishing opportunity. This state is called stagger. It does not last forever. Miss the opportunity and the enemy may resume fighting. You have a moment to decide whether to close in. The enemy briefly flashes and staggers, creating a short opening to approach. Wait too long and that opportunity can disappear.

Finish at close range with a Glory Kill and receive resource rewards. This provides more than a tool for attacking: it gives a reason to take the risk of approaching. Getting needed recovery when health is low helps encourage forward movement. Watch the blue health drops after the finisher and the health increase that follows. Reducing the enemy count also supplies resources for the next exchange.

The presentation also explains how low health can affect the recovery reward. The rules for granting health and ammunition are designed separately. Design each resource type and reward amount separately. This helps avoid a solution that repeats only one action. A healthy player and a wounded player do not need exactly the same reward. Separate the rules that help restore health from those that provide ammunition.

When ammunition runs short, the chainsaw provides a large resource reward. It costs fuel in return. Even actions that obtain resources have a cost. The chainsaw’s ammunition reward requires fuel. When ammunition runs low, the chainsaw and its remaining fuel become relevant. It is a resource trade-off, rather than a free recovery button.

Create a stagger opportunity, close in for a finish, and use the rewards to keep fighting. DOOM creates a path to recovery during combat. To survive, move forward: the rewards support that behavior. The reward for approaching danger creates room for the next attack.

How Breath of the Wild connects fire, water, and electricity

Whenever fire and water meet, must a developer script a separate scene? Breath of the Wild combines shared state rules, allowing players to discover unexpected solutions.

In Breath of the Wild, grass catches fire, and rain puts it out. Were these combinations authored separately for every scene? Motion calculations and state rules such as ignition are distinct. Watch this patch of grass catch fire.

The system that handles these shared state changes is the Chemistry Engine. Physics calculates the motion of objects. The chemistry engine handles burning and extinguished states. The state system distinguishes elements from materials. Elements such as fire and materials such as wood are assigned relationships. Although it is called a chemistry engine, it does not simulate all real-world chemistry.

The first rule: an element changes a material’s state. Fire touching wood changes it into a burning state. A shared rule can apply in many locations. The actual footage also shows ice shrinking near a fire. Distinguish pushing an object to a new position from changing its state through heat.

The second rule: elements can also change other elements’ states. The relationship between water and fire is handled within the same framework. Beyond checking only burning objects, effects themselves can interact. In the explanatory model, bring water into contact with the fire. The fire effect itself is affected by another effect.

The third rule concerns relationships between materials. Within these chemistry rules, they do not directly change each other’s states. That does not mean they cannot collide. This distinguishes motion physics from the rules for state changes. Two wooden boxes can collide and move without setting one another on fire. Being pushed by a collision and changing a chemical state are different layers.

Treat rain as the water element, and the existing water–fire relationship can be reused. Electricity and wind also belong within the game’s simplified state model. As more rules can be combined, players gain more combinations to try. The same relationship between fire and water can be reused in a different patch of grass. Instead of scripting a separate result for each location, define relationships between materials and effects.

Use physics for movement and shared rules for state changes. Setting a fire and extinguishing it with rain feel like consistent rules of one world. The goal is more than producing many events: create relationships that can be recombined. In short, physics handles motion; chemistry handles state changes.
